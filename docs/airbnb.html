This is not
home sharing

36,561 active listings across 48 Buenos Aires neighbourhoods, scraped April 2024 —
read against the question a prospective host should ask first: who am I competing with?

Executive summary

The city has 36,561 active listings held by 18,300 hosts. Three quarters of those hosts own exactly one property — and together they account for only 38.2% of the inventory.

The remaining 61.8% belongs to hosts running more than one listing, and 42 hosts alone — two tenths of one per cent of all hosts — control 3,948 properties, almost 11% of the market. The five largest operate 294, 243, 181, 170 and 158 listings respectively.

Anyone entering this market as an individual owner is not competing with neighbours renting a spare room. They are competing with property management companies, and the operating implications follow from that.

Where the market is, and what it costs

Palermo and Recoleta alone hold 46.7% of every listing in the city — 17,061 properties between two neighbourhoods out of forty-eight. Palermo by itself is 11,781 listings, roughly a third of Buenos Aires.

Going from two guests to four lifts the nightly rate by 43% while lowering the price per guest to the cheapest point on the curve. Above four, the per-guest price climbs again: a six-sleeper earns more per night but competes less efficiently. A four-guest apartment is simultaneously the best value for the traveller and the strongest step up in revenue for the owner.

Superhost buys volume, not price

41.5% of hosts hold Superhost status, which is high. The return on it is not where owners usually assume.

Superhost31,4724.8720

The price premium is 5.6% — barely visible. The review count is five times higher. Superhost status is not a licence to charge more; it is a booking-volume mechanism. Pricing strategy built on the badge will disappoint; occupancy strategy built on it will not.

A quarter of the market is asleep

10,091 listings — 27.6% of the city — received no reviews at all in the previous twelve months. In the calendar sample, 10.2% of properties were never occupied across the year while 13.2% were blocked or booked more than 95% of it.

The market is bimodal rather than average: a large dormant tail alongside a professionally run core. Headline competition numbers overstate the field a new listing actually faces.

What we would do

  1. Target four-guest apartments. It is the best revenue-per-competitiveness point in the dataset, and it is under-supplied relative to two-guest stock by better than two to one.
  2. Do not buy into Palermo on price. A third of the city's supply sits there for a premium of roughly 1.5× over cheaper barrios. Belgrano and Colegiales hold similar rates against far less competition.
  3. Pursue Superhost for occupancy, not for rate. Budget for the reviews, not for a price rise that the data says is under six per cent.
  4. Discount the competition count. With 27.6% of listings inactive, the real field is closer to 26,000 than 36,500.
  5. Resolve the licensing position before investing. 98.8% of listings carry no licence number. That is a regulatory exposure sitting across the whole market, not an individual oversight.

Method

Inside Airbnb extract for Buenos Aires, scraped 29 April 2024: 36,561 listings across 75 fields, plus a forward-looking availability calendar. Prices analysed as medians rather than means throughout — the distribution is heavily right-skewed, with a maximum forty times the 95th percentile, and a mean would be meaningless. Neighbourhood comparisons restricted to barrios with 150 or more listings.

What this does not tell you

The calendar file is truncated. It contains exactly 1,048,575 rows — one short of Excel's maximum — and covers 2,873 of the 36,561 listings, ordered by listing ID rather than sampled at random. Every occupancy figure here is drawn from that 7.9% subset and should be treated as directional only. Re-exporting the calendar outside Excel would resolve it.

Occupancy is inferred, not observed. A blocked date in the calendar may be booked or simply unavailable; the two cannot be distinguished, so occupancy is an upper bound.

Prices are in Argentine pesos at April 2024, with inflation then running close to 300% a year and several parallel exchange rates in circulation. The median of 30,598 converts to somewhere near USD 30–35 depending on which rate is applied. These figures do not compare across time and should not be projected forward.

Listed price is also the advertised rate, not revenue: cleaning fees, discounts for longer stays and platform commission are not captured. And the empty licence field records what hosts declared to the platform, which is not proof of non-compliance.
